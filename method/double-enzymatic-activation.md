# The Double Enzymatic Activation Method

A summary. The name is grander than the practice: the dough gets **two cold rests**, and the
first one is the whole trick.

## The idea in one paragraph

Flour enzymes keep working in the fridge. Yeast and lactic acid bacteria mostly do not. If you
mix a dough and refrigerate it immediately, amylase spends the night snipping starch into
maltose while nothing ferments and the pH stays high, which keeps protease (the enzyme that
weakens gluten) nearly idle. The next day the dough is extensible, sugar-rich, and ready. Bulk
fermentation is faster than usual, the crumb opens easily, and the leftover sugar browns the
crust hard.

## The weekly rhythm

| Step | When | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Feed 2–3 small levains | A flexible day, e.g. Sunday | 15 min, then fridge |
| Wake one levain in the recipe's flour and water | Whenever you bake | ~1 h |
| Mix final dough, straight into the fridge | Same evening or morning | 8–16 h |
| Bulk with folds | Day of bake | 3–4 h |
| Shape, optional cold proof, bake | Whenever it suits | flexible |

## Cold-fed levain

- Feed 25 g starter with 25 g flour and 25 g water. Add a pinch of whole wheat or rye for
  enzymatic fuel. Let it peak, 6 to 8 hours at room temperature.
- Divide into as many jars as you want levains, feed each 1:2:2, ferment overnight.
- Next morning, when peaked, give each jar a small feed (about 20 g flour and 20 g water) and
  refrigerate.
- Stir once a day if you remember. Use within about five days; two to four days is the sweet spot.

**Is it still good?** Mild tang and a bready smell means yes. A little separation is fine.
Solvent or nail-polish smell means it needs a feed first.

## The bake

1. **Wake-up.** Weigh the recipe water into a bowl and dissolve the cold levain in it. Whisk in
   enough of the flour to make a thick batter, roughly equal flour and water by weight. Put the
   salt on top of the remaining flour so you do not forget it. Wait about an hour, or until you
   see bubbles.
2. **Final mix.** Add the remaining flour and salt. Mix until no dry flour remains. No kneading.
3. **First cold rest.** Cover and refrigerate for 8 to 16 hours. This is the enzymatic rest.
4. **Bulk.** Take the dough out, let it sit 30 to 60 minutes, then one stretch-and-fold for
   strength followed by 3 or 4 gentle coil folds spaced 45 to 60 minutes apart. Use a wide,
   shallow container: it equalises temperature faster and lets gas spread.
5. **Shape and proof.** Do what the recipe says. A second cold rest after shaping relaxes the
   gluten and concentrates sugar at the surface, which gives blisters and colour.
6. **Bake with steam, finish dry.** Steam keeps the crust supple while the loaf expands. Dry heat
   afterwards drives browning.

## Levers

| Want | Do |
|---|---|
| More open crumb | Raise hydration toward 78–82% |
| More flavour | Add 5–10% whole wheat or rye. Use a levain that has sat a few days. |
| Faster | More levain (15–20%) or a warmer bulk |
| Slower, more flexible | Less levain (10%) and chill right after mixing |
| Buttery, mild | Keep the early bulk warm (25–28 °C) so diacetyl forms before the pH drops |
| Sharper tang | Longer cold phases, stiffer levain |

## Things that go wrong

- **Dough is slack and tears after the second cold rest.** Protease had too long. Shorten the
  first cold rest next time, or skip the post-shape cold proof.
- **Crust burns before the inside is done.** The dough is full of sugar. Drop the oven 10 to
  20 °C and bake a little longer, or tent with foil once the colour is right.
- **Bland or dense.** Underfermented. The levain may have been too old or the kitchen too cold.
  Give bulk more time and warmth, and watch the dough rather than the clock.
